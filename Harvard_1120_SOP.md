Statement of Purpose
Program: Master of Engineering (ME) in Computational Science and Engineering
Applicant: Yang Sen (Liam) Lin
In my recent research accepted to CoRL 2025, titled “See, Point, Fly,” I successfully achieved state-of-the-art zero-shot UAV navigation by geometrically decoupling vision from control. While this modular approach was effective, I encountered a critical computational deadlock: geometric rules are inherently brittle in dynamic, unstructured environments, yet the alternative—end-to-end Generative Visual-Language-Action (VLA) models—is currently too computationally heavy for real-time edge control. This experience clarified that the future of Physical AI requires satisfying two opposing constraints: the agile adaptability of generative reasoning and the extreme efficiency of embedded systems.
I am applying to the Master of Engineering (ME) in Computational Science and Engineering at Harvard to bridge this gap. My specific research objective is to investigate generative control policies that are mathematically robust yet algorithmically optimized for resource-constrained deployment. I aim to achieve this by utilizing the 2-year thesis timeline to integrate Prof. Yilun Du’s compositional generative frameworks with Prof. Vijay Janapa Reddi’s edge computing expertise.
My journey toward this “computational bridge” began with a focus on algorithmic rigor. For my CoRL work, I derived a geometric action-to-control framework to map 2D VLM prompts to 3D metric controls. Leveraging my background in Automatic Control (I) & (II) (A+/A), I designed a closed-loop Adaptive Step System inspired by PID principles that solved depth ambiguity without depth sensors. Mathematically, I optimized the visual tracking complexity from O(N2) to O(N) via heuristic sparse sampling, enabling the system to run on standard hardware. However, despite these optimizations, the system relies on deterministic geometric priors. It fails when the environment requires non-linear reasoning, such as dodging erratic obstacles. To enable true autonomy, I must move from deterministic geometry to Stochastic Generative Policies, which requires the deep theoretical foundation in Energy-Based Models offered by the CSE curriculum.
Simultaneously, I recognized that advanced algorithms are useless if they saturate onboard hardware. Dur- ing my internship at Wolley Inc., I engineered C-based diagnostic tools to profile Memory Bandwidth Pressure for CXL server interconnects. While CXL is a server-scale technology, the fundamental bottle- neck—managing memory bandwidth under high-throughput constraints—is exactly what plagues VLM deployment on embedded drones. This experience gave me the low-level architectural intuition needed to optimize I/O efficiency for physical AI. I plan to apply this optimization mindset during my upcoming internship at OMRON SINIC X, where I will distill Robotics Foundation Models for soft robotics. Yet, I realize that software distillation alone is insufficient; deploying foundation models on drones requires a rigorous investigation into hardware-software co-design, a core focus of the CSE program.
The Harvard CSE ME is the only program that combines rigorous mathematical modeling with high- performance computing, offering the ideal environment to merge these distinct research tracks over a dedicated thesis. The curriculum is the rigorous toolkit I need. My strong performance in Engineering Mathematics (I) & (II) (A-/A-), Fluid Mechanics (A+), and Heat Transfer (A+) has grounded me in the differential equation rigor required for AM 205 (Numerical Methods). Furthermore, AM 207 (Stochastic Methods) and CS 2050 (High Performance Computing) provide the necessary probabilistic and systems foundations to transition my research from geometric to generative models.
Research-wise, I seek to work with Prof. Yilun Du to upgrade my geometric approach to a Generative Policy approach, exploring how Energy-Based Models can capture complex dynamics. Simultaneously, I aim to collaborate with Prof. Vijay Janapa Reddi to investigate the trade-offs of applying TinyML quantization to these diffusion policies, determining if we can retain expressivity while meeting the millisecond-latency requirements of a drone. The Master of Engineering thesis is the necessary vehicle to integrate these disparate fields—Stochastic Algorithms and Edge Systems—over a dedicated 2-year timeline.
My long-term goal is to lead R&D in Physical AI, solving the scalability of autonomous agents. Harvard CSE will transform me from a Robotics Engineer who builds systems into a Computational Scientist capable of inventing the mathematical frameworks that define the next generation of autonomy.
