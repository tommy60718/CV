In my recent research accepted to \textbf{CoRL 2025}, titled \emph{See, Point, Fly}, I identified a fundamental trade-off in the current state of robotic autonomy: geometric rules provide reliability but become brittle in dynamic environments; conversely, Generative Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models offer adaptability but suffer from hallucinations and high computational latency. My objective at Stanford is to resolve this tension by investigating the \textbf{mathematical interfaces} between rigid control theory and probabilistic foundation models. By integrating the safety-aware control frameworks of \textbf{Prof. Dorsa Sadigh’s ILIAD Lab} with the generalist robot learning of \textbf{Prof. Chelsea Finn’s IRIS Lab}, I aim to architect ``Trustworthy Embodied Systems'' that constrain the creativity of VLMs with the safety guarantees of control theory.

My journey toward this objective began with a focus on algorithmic decoupling. In my CoRL 2025 work, I formulated a geometric projection algorithm to ground 2D VLM prompts into 3D metric controls, solving depth ambiguity via pin-hole camera models without external sensors. By framing action prediction as a spatial grounding task rather than end-to-end text generation, I achieved state-of-the-art success rates (\textbf{93.9\%}) in unstructured environments. However, through rigorous testing, I observed that my ``learning-free'' geometric priors became brittle when facing erratic, dynamic obstacles where non-linear reasoning is required. To overcome this \textbf{``reactivity bottleneck''} caused by VLM inference latency, I realized I must evolve from static heuristics to \textbf{Generalist Learning Policies}. I specifically require the curriculum at Stanford to investigate how meta-learning and stochastic control can replace static rules, enabling drones to adapt to non-linear dynamics in milliseconds.

Simultaneously, my research philosophy is defined by the constraints of the physical layer. While many researchers focus solely on algorithmic performance, my internship at \textbf{Wolley Inc.} grounded me in the realities of hardware architecture. There, I engineered C-based diagnostic tools to profile \textbf{CXL memory bandwidth} and host pressure. This experience gave me the low-level architectural intuition needed to optimize I/O efficiency for physical AI, distinguishing my approach as a full-stack system builder. I plan to extend this hardware-software co-design mindset during my upcoming internship at \textbf{OMRON SINIC X}, where I will distill Robotics Foundation Models for edge deployment. I recognized early on that algorithmic distillation is insufficient without optimizing the entire stack—from memory protocols to control loops—making the \textbf{Hardware/Software Systems} depth area at Stanford the ideal academic home for my work.

My academic preparation has been deliberately structured to support this rigorous interdisciplinary work. \textbf{I demonstrated graduate-level competency by securing an A+ in \emph{Computer Vision}, proving my ability to thrive in research-led curricula.} Beyond coursework, I have actively applied advanced mathematics to solve engineering problems: I utilized \textbf{Projective Geometry} to derive non-linear unprojection laws in my CoRL paper and applied \textbf{Vector Calculus} to decompose displacement vectors into velocity primitives. This practical application of geometric modeling has prepared me for the rigorous theoretical training of \textbf{EE 263 (Linear Dynamical Systems)} and \textbf{EE 364A (Convex Optimization)}. I specifically require \textbf{EE 364A} to learn how to formulate safety constraints as optimization layers, and \textbf{CS 330 (Meta-Learning)} to master the few-shot adaptation techniques necessary to move beyond my current zero-shot frameworks.

My technical roadmap directly maps to two specific research groups at Stanford. I aim to contribute to \textbf{Prof. Chelsea Finn’s IRIS Lab}, where I hope to apply my Sim2Real background to fine-tune generalist policies using large-scale interaction data, addressing the generalization limits I encountered in my CoRL work. Simultaneously, I seek to collaborate with \textbf{Prof. Dorsa Sadigh (ILIAD Lab)} to develop ``safety filters''—similar to the geometric constraints I derived—that use control-theoretic guarantees to prevent VLM hallucination in safety-critical tasks.

Beyond the laboratory, I carry an ``optimization mindset'' from code to community. As the \textbf{GDSC Chapter Lead}, I directed 24 members to bridge the gap between academic theory and industry practice, organizing workshops that impacted over 40 student developers. I aim to bring this leadership to the Stanford community, fostering collaboration between EE and CS cohorts. My long-term goal is to lead the R\&D of \textbf{Ubiquitous Physical AI}. The Stanford MSEE will transform me from a robotics engineer who builds working prototypes into a \textbf{Computational Systems Architect} capable of defining the safety guarantees required to deploy autonomous agents at a global scale.
